Water disruption — storage, treatment, conservation

When the tap is off, the pressure has dropped, or a Boil-Water Notice is in effect. A household budget for one week.

Urban Resilience rpi-hub v1.2 · field card
Review before field use. Field cards are condensed references, not certified curricula. Verify against your local protocols and have a qualified responder review before relying on them in an emergency.

Decision in 30 seconds

  1. Capture what is in your building. Fill bathtubs, sinks, pots, and clean food-grade containers while pressure is still in the lines.
  2. Treat before drinking. Boil 1 minute (3 min above 2,000 m) or chemically disinfect (see general-purpose water-purification card).
  3. Budget by use. Drinking and cooking first; hygiene second; flushing last. One gallon (~4 L) per person per day for drinking, cooking, basic hygiene; double in heat.

Detail

Storage

  • Food-grade containers only. Old soda or water bottles work; bleach jugs do not.
  • Pre-event: store 1 gal / person / day for at least 3 days, preferably 14.
  • Rotate every 6 months; cool, dark, off bare concrete (label leaches at warm temps).
  • Water-heater tank holds 30–50 gal — close the inlet valve when pressure drops, then drain from the bottom valve as needed. Confirm power / gas is off to the heater before draining.

Treatment quick-pick

  • Boil — most reliable, requires fuel.
  • Unscented bleach (5–6 % NaOCl) — 2 drops/L clear water, 4 drops/L cloudy. Stir. Wait 30 min.
  • Filter to 0.2 µm — bacteria/protozoa only; combine with bleach for viral cover.

Use priorities (descending)

  1. Drinking and cooking — treated.
  2. Brushing teeth — treated.
  3. Handwashing — treated for food prep; untreated grey water acceptable for general hand rinse if followed by hand sanitiser.
  4. Dishwashing — treated final rinse; grey water for first wash.
  5. Body hygiene — wet wipes and a quart of warm treated water go far.
  6. Flushing — bathtub or grey water, one bucket directly into the bowl forces a flush without using the tank.

Conservation moves

  • Cook one-pot meals; use the broth.
  • Re-wear clothes; spot-clean.
  • Wipe plates before washing.
  • Catch the cold-tap-warming-up water for cooking, not wasted.

Do not

Do not drink from a water heater, radiator, pool, or hot tub. Heaters can be safe but only if the inlet was closed and the tank is plumbed for potable supply; pools and hot tubs carry biocides and disinfection by-products in concentrations not safe to drink even after boiling. Toilet-tank water is also off-limits if any cleaning tablet has been used.

Signal that you are out of scope

You have an infant on formula, anyone immunocompromised, or anyone on dialysis or medications requiring high water intake; or you have no treatment method available at all. Contact your utility, public-health line, or shelter — bottled water is typically distributed during extended urban disruptions. Field treatment removes microbiological hazards only; chemical contamination from a watermain break under industrial ground requires waiting for utility clearance.
Sources: CDC "Creating and Storing an Emergency Water Supply" and "Drinking Water Advisories"; EPA "Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water"; FEMA Ready.gov water-storage guidance; Wikipedia "Boil-water advisory".
