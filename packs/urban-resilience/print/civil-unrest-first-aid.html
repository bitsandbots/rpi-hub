Civil unrest — first aid for tear gas, blunt trauma, crowd injury

Field medical response to three common injury types in dense crowd environments. Not legal or tactical guidance.

Urban Resilience rpi-POD v1.2 · field card
Review before field use. Field cards are condensed references, not certified curricula. Verify against your local protocols and have a qualified responder review before relying on them in an emergency.

Decision in 30 seconds

  1. Move to a safer space first. First aid in an active crowd is itself dangerous — extract patient and yourself to a calm side street, building lobby, or shielded area before treating.
  2. Triage life threats. Airway, breathing, severe bleeding before anything else.
  3. Limit your scope. Field care is irrigation, pressure, recognise-and-refer. Definitive care is a hospital.

Detail

Tear gas / pepper spray exposure

  • Move uphill and upwind out of the cloud. Face the wind, eyes closed and forward.
  • Do not rub eyes. Do not splash with milk-based mixtures (not sterile, contamination risk).
  • Irrigate eyes with copious clean water from the inner corner outward, head tilted so runoff does not cross the other eye. 15+ minutes for sustained relief.
  • Skin: rinse with cool water, then wash with mild soap. Remove and bag contaminated clothing.
  • Asthma or COPD: get away from the cloud immediately, use rescue inhaler, seek care for any breathing difficulty that does not resolve in minutes.

Blunt trauma — concussion screen (WHO/CDC symptom list)

  • Loss of consciousness (any duration).
  • Confusion, repeating questions, difficulty remembering events around the impact.
  • Headache that worsens.
  • Repeated vomiting.
  • Slurred speech, unsteady walking, weakness or numbness in a limb.
  • Unequal pupils.
  • Seizure.
  • Any of the above — treat as suspected traumatic brain injury, do not let the person be alone, refer to emergency care.

Open wounds + fractures

  • Bleeding — direct pressure, 10 min by the clock. Tourniquet only for life-threatening extremity bleeding that pressure cannot control.
  • Suspected fracture — splint as found, do not realign. Pad pressure points. Check pulse / sensation distal to the injury before and after splinting.

Crowd crush

  • Crowd-crush asphyxia kills standing — chest compression by neighbours prevents inhalation, not falling.
  • If caught in a crush: arms across chest in a boxer stance to protect lungs; move sideways and diagonally with the flow, never against; conserve breath.
  • If you find a casualty: move to a shielded area, lie flat on back, open airway (head-tilt / chin-lift if no spinal concern, otherwise jaw-thrust), check breathing. CPR if pulseless and a trained provider is present.

Do not

Do not flush eyes with milk, antacid mixtures, or solvents — clean water is the consensus rinse for chemical-irritant exposure. Do not realign suspected fractures in the field. Do not move a suspected spinal injury unless the surrounding environment is more dangerous than the move; if you must, log-roll with the head and spine kept in line.

Signal that you are out of scope

Any of the concussion symptoms above; loss of consciousness even briefly; suspected spinal injury; airway compromise; persistent shortness of breath; blunt impact to head, chest, abdomen, or pelvis with progressive pain; open fracture; bleeding not controlled by direct pressure within 10 minutes; pregnancy with abdominal trauma; eye injury with vision change. All require hospital evaluation. Field care is to stabilise and extract; do not assume because someone walks and talks now that they are uninjured.
Sources: WHO "Guidelines for the medical management of chemical casualties" and STEPS injury-surveillance materials; CDC "Concussion / Traumatic Brain Injury" and chemical-irritant guidance; American Red Cross First Aid; Wikipedia "Tear gas", "Crowd collapses and crushes", "Concussion".
