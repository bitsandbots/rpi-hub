Wound care — irrigate, debride, dress

First-aid management of a contaminated open wound when definitive care is delayed.

General Purpose SIGNAL v1.2 · field card
Review before field use. Field cards are condensed references, not certified curricula. Verify against your local protocols and have a qualified responder review before relying on them in an emergency.

Decision in 30 seconds

  1. Stop the bleed. Direct pressure with the cleanest cloth available for 10 full minutes by the clock. Do not lift to peek.
  2. Irrigate. Once bleeding is controlled, flush with clean, potable water under pressure for at least one minute.
  3. Cover. Dry skin around the wound, apply a non-adherent dressing, secure loosely. Re-check in 24 hours.

Detail

Bleeding control

  • Direct pressure first. Maintain without releasing.
  • Elevate the limb above the heart if no fracture is suspected.
  • If pressure alone fails on an extremity and bleeding is life-threatening, apply a tourniquet 5–8 cm above the wound (not over a joint), tighten until bleeding stops, note the time.
  • Pack deep junctional wounds (groin, armpit) with clean gauze and maintain manual pressure.

Irrigation + dressing

  • Use potable water or sterile saline. A syringe or a punctured plastic bottle delivers useful pressure.
  • Aim 0.5–1 L per wound for visible contamination. More for road grit or bites.
  • Remove only loose debris with clean tweezers. Leave embedded objects in place.
  • Apply petrolatum gauze or a non-stick pad, then absorbent gauze, then tape or wrap. Snug, not tight — check pulse and capillary refill distal to the dressing.
  • Change dressings daily or when soaked through.

Do not

Do not pour hydrogen peroxide, alcohol, or iodine into an open wound. These damage the tissue that needs to heal and slow closure. Clean water under pressure outperforms antiseptics for wound irrigation in field conditions.

Signal that you are out of scope

Seek a higher-trained responder for: bleeding not controlled after 10 minutes of direct pressure; deep puncture wounds; animal or human bites; wounds over a joint, on the face, or wider than 2 cm gaping; embedded foreign body that cannot be flushed out; signs of infection at 24–48 h (spreading redness, warmth, pus, fever, red streaks toward the heart); patient without tetanus coverage in the last 10 years for a dirty wound.
Sources: WHO "Prehospital trauma care systems"; CDC "Emergency Wound Management"; American Red Cross First Aid guidance; Wikipedia "Wound healing" and "Tourniquet".
