Pacific Northwest edible plants (and lookalikes)

Four well-known PNW species, with the dangerous lookalikes that cause most poisoning calls.

Pacific Northwest rpi-hub v1.2 · field card
Review before field use. Field cards are condensed references, not certified curricula. Verify against your local protocols and have a qualified responder review before relying on them in an emergency.

Decision in 30 seconds

  1. Identify three independent characteristics — leaf, stem, and reproductive part (flower, berry, cone) — before eating anything.
  2. If any one feature is wrong, the plant is not what you think. Discard.
  3. Eat a small test portion first (a few berries; a single thumb-sized piece) and wait two hours before eating more, even for known species.

Detail

Foraging warning. Misidentified plants kill more foragers in the Pacific Northwest than any other natural cause. Water hemlock and death camas are present across the region and have killed experienced wild-food gatherers. Never eat any plant on the basis of a single matching feature. When in doubt, do not eat it — calories are recoverable; alkaloid poisoning often is not.
EdibleIdentify byDangerous lookalikeLookalike tell
Salmonberry (Rubus spectabilis) Compound leaf of 3 leaflets; pink flowers in early spring; orange-to-red soft raspberry-like berry on a thorned cane. None highly toxic in same form, but unripe or mouldy berries cause GI upset. Reject white-coated or wilted berries.
Stinging nettle (Urtica dioica) — cooked young tops only Opposite, serrated leaves on a square stem covered in stinging hairs; small green flower clusters. Wood nettle and false nettle (edible but less common); confusion with horsenettle (Solanum) on disturbed ground. Horsenettle has alternate leaves, star-shaped purple flowers, and yellow tomato-like berries — do not eat.
Camas (Camassia quamash) — bulb, cooked thoroughly Long grass-like leaves from base; spike of blue six-petalled flowers in late spring. Death camas (Zigadenus / Toxicoscordion venenosus) — fatal in small amounts. Death camas has cream-white flowers, not blue. Only forage camas in bloom when the flower colour is visible. Bulbs out of bloom are indistinguishable to a non-expert — do not dig.
Wild carrot / Queen Anne's lace (Daucus carota) — root from first-year plant Finely divided fern-like leaves; carrot smell when crushed; hairy stem; flat white flower umbel with a single dark central floret. Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) and water hemlock (Cicuta douglasii) — both deadly. Hemlocks have smooth, hairless stems with purple blotches and a foul (mousey) smell. No carrot smell. No central dark floret. If the stem is hairless, do not eat.

Do not

Do not forage any plant in the carrot family (Apiaceae) without absolute certainty — water hemlock is the most toxic plant in North America and grows in wet ditches across the PNW. Do not eat any bulb from a camas-like plant that is not currently in flower. Do not rely on smell, taste, or "universal edibility" tests — they are unreliable for alkaloid poisons.

Signal that you are out of scope

Suspected poisoning — vomiting, salivation, tingling, seizures, confusion — within 1 to 4 hours of eating a foraged plant. Save a sample of the plant if possible. This is poison-control territory; do not induce vomiting unless instructed by a poison control centre or a clinician. Any time you are not 100 % confident on all three identification features above, you are out of scope as a forager.
