Altitude sickness — recognise + descend

Acute Mountain Sickness, HACE, HAPE. Recognition first; descent is the only universally effective treatment.

Mountain West rpi-POD v1.2 · field card
Review before field use. Field cards are condensed references, not certified curricula. Verify against your local protocols and have a qualified responder review before relying on them in an emergency.

Decision in 30 seconds

  1. Recognise. Any combination of headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, or poor sleep at altitudes above ~2,500 m (~8,000 ft).
  2. Stop ascending. Rest at current altitude, hydrate, treat headache with paracetamol/ibuprofen at standard doses if otherwise tolerated.
  3. If symptoms worsen, descend. Even 300–500 m loss often resolves AMS. Severe symptoms (see below) mean descend now — do not wait for daylight or rescue.

Detail

SyndromeRecogniseAction
AMS (Acute Mountain Sickness) Headache plus one of: nausea, fatigue, dizziness, sleep disturbance. Onset 6–24 h after ascent above ~2,500 m. Stop ascent. Rest, hydrate, treat headache. Reassess in 24 h. Descend if no improvement.
HACE (High-Altitude Cerebral Edema) AMS plus altered mental status (confusion, irrational behaviour) or ataxia (cannot walk heel-to-toe in a straight line). Life-threatening. Descend immediately, even at night. 500–1,000 m minimum. Continuous companion supervision.
HAPE (High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema) Breathlessness at rest, dry cough progressing to pink frothy sputum, gurgling/crackles in chest, fatigue out of proportion. Cyanosis. Life-threatening. Descend immediately. Sit upright, keep warm. Supplemental oxygen if available.

Prevention

  • Ascend slowly above 2,500 m: sleep no more than ~500 m higher than the previous night; build in a rest day every 1,000 m of sleeping-altitude gain.
  • Hydrate; avoid alcohol and sedatives for the first 48 hours at altitude.
  • Recognise that fitness does not prevent altitude illness — previous tolerance is the best predictor.
  • Prescription prophylaxis (e.g. acetazolamide) is available — this is a clinician's decision, not a field card decision.

Do not

Do not continue ascending while symptomatic, do not leave a symptomatic person alone, and do not use sleep aids that suppress breathing at altitude. Climbing higher with AMS is the most common path from a recoverable illness to HACE or HAPE.

Signal that you are out of scope

Any altered mental state, ataxia, breathlessness at rest, frothy sputum, or chest crackles — descend without delay and call for evacuation if available. Drug therapy beyond paracetamol/ibuprofen for headache is a clinician decision; do not initiate prescription medication from a field card. Pregnancy, sickle-cell trait, recent cardiac or pulmonary illness — altitude planning should involve a clinician before the trip, not after symptoms appear.
